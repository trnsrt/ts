The collapse of Terra wasn't an isolated earthquake; it was the shockwave that triggered a tsunami. The first domino had fallen, and its impact would send a chain reaction of failures across the entire crypto landscape.

The next to get [swept away](https://pontem.network/posts/the-collapse-of-3ac-three-arrows-capital-all-you-need-to-know) was Three Arrows Capital (3AC), one of the largest and most influential investment funds in the industry.

## How It Started {#how-it-started}

The contagion began with a single point of failure: UST, the Terra blockchain's algorithmic stablecoin. As a quick refresher, UST was a currency pegged to the US dollar not by cash reserves, but by a volatile sister token, LUNA. It was supercharged by the Anchor Protocol, which offered an irresistible 20% annual yield. This promise pulled in nearly $18 billion in just under a year.

This hormonal growth was obviously unsustainable. Insiders knew it and skeptics warned about it, yet as long as the music was playing, everyone kept dancing.

When the music finally stopped in May 2022, the industry frantically scrambled for a seat. But the most aggressive player on the dance floor, Three Arrows Capital, was left standing—and completely exposed. So, who were they?

## Meet Three Arrows Capital (3AC) {#meet-three-arrows-capital-3ac}

Founded in 2012 by two former traders, [Zhu Su](https://twitter.com/zhusu) and [Kyle Davies](https://twitter.com/kyleldavies), [Three Arrows Capital (3AC)](https://www.threearrowscap.com/about-us/) became a legend in the crypto bull market. At its core, it was an arbitrage fund—a firm designed to profit from small price differences between similar products in different markets.

But 3AC didn’t just play with small differences. They became famous for a few signature, high-conviction trades that they amplified with massive amounts of borrowed money.

### 3AC's Three Signature Plays {#b-3acs-three-signature-plays}

To understand 3AC's collapse, you have to understand the three trades that fueled its rise. Each seemed brilliant on the surface but concealed a fatal, illiquid flaw.

**1. The Grayscale Bitcoin Trust (GBTC) Trade**

Before the [US approved](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF12573/IF12573.2.pdf) Bitcoin ETFs in 2024, large institutions wanting BTC exposure had one main option: the Grayscale Bitcoin Trust (GBTC). This exclusive access created huge demand, causing GBTC shares to trade at a significant *premium* to the actual Bitcoin they held.

The trade was simple: 3AC would borrow Bitcoin, deposit it into the trust to create new GBTC shares, wait out a six-month lock-up period, and then sell those shares on the open market for a handsome premium. The catch? You couldn't redeem your GBTC for the underlying Bitcoin. The only way out was to sell to another buyer.

This was fine as long as the premium existed. But what if it vanished and became a *discount*? Then your capital was trapped. Getting out at a loss was the only option unless the trust could convert into an ETF—a move the SEC resisted [until a court forced its hand](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/whats-stake-grayscales-spot-bitcoin-etf-case-against-sec-2023-08-29/).

**2. The Staked ETH (stETH) Trade**

When Ethereum moved to Proof-of-Stake (PoS) in September 2022, users could lock up their ETH to secure the network and earn interest. But this ETH was [locked until at least April 2023](https://blog.ethereum.org/2023/03/28/shapella-mainnet-announcement).

[Lido Finance](https://lido.fi/) offered a solution: give them your ETH, and they’d give you stETH in return. This "liquid staking derivative" lets you earn staking rewards while still being able to use the stETH token across DeFi. Theoretically, 1 stETH should equal 1 ETH. But again, there was a catch. You couldn't redeem your stETH for ETH until the network upgrade. Your only exit was the open market. If too many people tried to sell at once, the price of stETH would "de-peg" and trade at a discount to ETH.

**3. The UST "Risk-Free" Yield Trade**

This was 3AC's most direct bet on the Terra ecosystem. As we covered, Anchor Protocol was paying 20% interest on UST deposits. In a world where you could borrow stablecoins like USDC for less than 4%, the math was intoxicating. 3AC borrowed heavily to pour money into this trade, chasing what looked like a money-printing machine. Buyer happy, seller happy.

Until the music stopped.

### How Leverage Turned a Bad Bet Into a Catastrophe {#how-leverage-turned-a-bad-bet-into-a-catastrophe}

3AC didn't just make these trades; they made them with staggering amounts of borrowed money. They took out massive loans, using their holdings of GBTC and stETH as collateral. This leverage was rocket fuel on the way up, but it became an accelerant in the fire on the way down.

When the markets turned, the hidden risks of these trades became terrifyingly real.

First, the UST peg broke. The "zero risk" trade exploded, punching a massive hole in 3AC's balance sheet.

This triggered the second stage of the collapse: the liquidity crunch. Spooked by the Terra implosion, 3AC's lenders demanded their money back. When 3AC couldn't pay, the lenders began a fire sale, dumping the GBTC and stETH they held as collateral onto the market.

This created a death spiral. The forced selling [widened](https://ycharts.com/companies/GBTC/discount_or_premium_to_nav) the GBTC discount to a staggering 34% and [pushed](https://www.trustnodes.com/2022/06/13/steth-drops-to-0-93-eth) stETH's [price](https://coinmarketcap.com/currencies/steth/steth/eth/) down to 0.93 ETH. The very assets 3AC held were plummeting in value precisely when they needed cash the most, triggering even more liquidations. In June 2022, BTC itself [lost nearly 40%](https://www.coindesk.com/markets/2022/07/01/brutal-month-for-bitcoin-as-june-ends-with-biggest-drop-in-11-years) of its value, erasing what was left of their collateral.

To make matters worse, allegations surfaced that 3AC had used the same collateral to get loans from multiple lenders—a practice that, if true, is fraud. In September 2023, the Monetary Authority of Singapore [banned](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/enforcement/enforcement-actions/2023/mas-issues-prohibition-orders-against-three-arrows-capital-zhu-su-and-kyle-livingston-davies) both founders from its capital markets for nine years, citing a 'flagrant disregard' for regulations.

The saga took another dramatic turn just weeks later. On September 29, 2023, Zhu Su was [arrested in Singapore'](https://www.finews.com/news/english-news/59522-three-arrows-co-founder-arrested-singapore)s airport while attempting to flee the country. He was sentenced to four months in prison for failing to cooperate with liquidators and was released in December 2023.
