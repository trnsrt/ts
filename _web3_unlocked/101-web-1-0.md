Let's start with a story you've probably heard bits and pieces of. The internet is, at its core, just [a massive network of computers talking to each other](https://www.usg.edu/galileo/skills/unit07/internet07_02.phtml). While the idea is nearly a century old, the first real computers in the 1950s laid the practical groundwork.

The internet’s real origin story, however, was forged in the Cold War. In the 1960s, the US military and government-funded universities began tackling a critical question: "In the event of a nuclear attack, how do we protect our most vital information?" The primary threat was centralization. If all your data is in one place, it only takes one hit to lose everything. The solution was clear: build a *decentralized structure*.

This thinking gave birth to the ARPANET project, [which led to the creation](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/arpanet-internet) of TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) and IP (Internet Protocol) in 1974[^1]. These protocols were the rules of the road for this new network.

At the same time, a critical piece of infrastructure emerged: the DNS (Domain Name Service). Designed in the early 1980s, it provided a system for turning human-readable domain names like [google.com](http://google.com) into network addresses. When the NSFNET project connected university supercomputers in 1986, the DNS became essential for navigating the rapidly growing network.

The web as we know it, the 'www', arrived in the late 1980s. Tim Berners-Lee, [a researcher at CERN](https://www.home.cern/science/computing/birth-web), saw scientists struggling to share information across different machines. His solution was a standardized system for linking documents, giving us the foundational trio of the modern internet: HTML, HTTP, and the URL.

## The Philosophy of Web 1.0 {#the-philosophy-of-web-1-0}

Web 1.0 wasn't about apps or social networks; it was about [building the fundamental rails](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/computer-science/web-10). This era was defined by a few core principles.

### Infrastructure Built to Last {#infrastructure-built-to-last}

Building a global communication network is a monumental task that demands a long-term vision. You have to design an architecture that can last for decades.

It's not something you can rush. Imagine trying to build the internet with venture capital. Investors want a return, and they want it fast. That model works for consumer products, but not for deep infrastructure. The digital ground we all stand on has to be stable.

You can't just say, "I designed it this way yesterday, but I'm changing it today." Thousands of applications depend on that foundation remaining predictable and solid.

### Open and Free by Design {#open-and-free-by-design}

That’s why the core protocols of Web 1.0 were built as open-source software, [free from licensing fees](https://www.w3.org/blog/2023/30th-anniversary-of-licensing-the-web-for-general-use-and-at-no-cost/). Think about it: you don't pay a toll to use HTTP or TCP/IP. Pretty remarkable, right?

This was a deliberate choice. Universities shared their research freely, allowing developers to build on each other's work instead of in their own separate bubbles.

This collaborative spirit created standardized protocols that could speak to each other. Without it, we would have faced a digital Tower of Babel—a fragmented world of incompatible networks. The internet as a unified, global platform would have been impossible.

### Clumsy by Modern Standards {#clumsy-by-modern-standards}

Let’s be honest: Web 1.0 was not user-friendly. Its creators were not focused on user experience; they were busy solving fundamental engineering problems. Anyone who remembers the screech of a dial-up modem and the agonizing wait to connect can attest to this. The sleek, intuitive applications we take for granted today were still a decade away, waiting for the next phase: Web 2.0.

### A Truly Distributed Network {#a-truly-distributed-network}

The internet began as a [connection of independent machines in different places](https://hackernoon.com/in-the-90s-web-10-was-running-on-a-fully-decentralized-infrastructure). Web 1.0 embodied this principle. Innovations came from universities across the US and Europe, were debated openly, and became standards through community consensus.

While a few institutions took the lead, the process itself was distributed. It was a network built by a community, for everyone—a core idea that would lie dormant for years, only to re-emerge with a vengeance in Web3.

[^1]: These protocols were implemented on ARPANET in 1983.
